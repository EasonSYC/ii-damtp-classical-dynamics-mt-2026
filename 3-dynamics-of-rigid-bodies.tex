\section{Dynamics of Rigid Bodies}

A \term{rigid body} is one which has no internal degrees of freedom. In particular, if the body consists of \(n\) particles at locations \(\vb{r}_i\) for \(i = 1, 2, \ldots, n\), then the relative separations \(\abs{\vb{r}_i - \vb{r}_j}\) are fixed for all \(i, j\).

To describe such a rigid body, we must specify
\begin{itemize}
    \item the \term{location} \(\vb{R}\pqty{t}\) of its centre of mass;
    \item its \term{orientation} around the centre of mass.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Rotations of a Rigid Body}

Consider a rigid body whose centre of mass is fixed at \(\vb{0} \in \RR^3\). Then the most general motion is a rotation \(R\pqty{t} \in \SO\pqty{3}\) around the origin. We can describe this using either the \term{space frame} \(\Bqty{\tilde{\vb{e}}_a}\) or a \term{body frame} \(\Bqty{\vb{e}_a}\), as shown in \autoref{fig:space-and-body-frames}.

The \term{space frame} is simply the standard basis of \(\RR^3\). A \term{body frame} is one whose axes are fixed in the body. The space frame will not change with time, while the body frame will rotate with the body (and remains fixed relative to the body). Regardless, both sets of axes are always orthonormal
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{\vb{e}}_a \cdot \tilde{\vb{e}}_b = \kronecker_{ab}, \quad
    \vb{e}_a\pqty{t} \cdot \vb{e}_b\pqty{t} = \kronecker_{ab}.
\end{equation}

Any point \(\vb{r} \pqty{t}\) in the body can be expanded in either basis. In the space frame, since the space frame is fixed, the components must change, while in the body frame, the components must be constant, i.e.
\begin{equation}
    \vb{r}\pqty{t} = \tilde{r}_a \pqty{t} \tilde{\vb{e}}_a = r_a \vb{e}_a\pqty{t}.
\end{equation}

The velocity is therefore given by
\begin{equation}
    \dv{\vb{r}}{t} = \dv{\tilde{r}_a}{t} \tilde{\vb{e}}_a = r_a \dv{\vb{e}_a}{t} = r_a \dv{R_{ab}\pqty{t}}{t} \tilde{\vb{e}}_b
\end{equation}
where \(R\pqty{t}\) is the rotation matrix that rotates the body frame into the space frame, i.e. \(\vb{e}_a\pqty{t} = R_{ab}\pqty{t} \tilde{\vb{e}}_b\). We can write this entirely in the body frame as
\begin{equation}
    \dv{\vb{e}_a}{t} = \dv{R_{ab}\pqty{t}}{t} \pqty{R\inverse}_{bc} \vb{e}_c = \omega_{ac} \vb{e}_c
\end{equation}
where we denote
\begin{equation}
    \omega = \dot{R} R\inverse.
\end{equation}

But note that \(R \in \SO\pqty{3}\), so \(R R \transpose = I\), and hence \(\dot{R} R\transpose + R \dot{R}\transpose = O\). Hence,
\begin{equation}
    \omega\transpose = \pqty{\dot{R} R\transpose}\transpose = R \dot{R}\transpose = - \dot{R} R\transpose = - \omega,
\end{equation}
so \(\omega\) is an \emph{anti-symmetric} \(3 \times 3\) matrix. This allows us to define a vector \(\vb*{\omega}\), such that
\begin{equation}
    \omega_a = \frac{1}{2} \levicivita_{abc} \omega_{bc} \iff \omega_{ab} = \levicivita_{abc} \omega_c.
\end{equation}

Putting this together, we have
\begin{equation}
    \dv{\vb{e}_a}{t} = \omega_{ab} \vb{e}_b = \levicivita_{abc} \omega_c \vb{e}_b = \vb*{\omega} \cross \vb{e}_a,
\end{equation}
and hence,
\begin{equation}
    \dot{\vb{r}} = \vb*{\omega} \cross \vb{r}
\end{equation}
where our \(\vb*{\omega}\) is measured in the body frame.

This means that
\begin{equation}
    \delta \vb{r} = \delta \phi \pqty{\vb{n} \cross \vb{r}},
\end{equation}
if the body rotates by \(\delta \phi\) around \(\vb{n}\) in time \(\delta t\). So the vector \(\vb*{\omega} \pqty{t}\) is the \term{angular velocity} of the body that we are familiar with, as shown in \autoref{fig:angular-velocity-vector}.

\begin{figure}[ht!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{fig/angular-velocity-vector.png}
    \caption{Angular Velocity Vector}
    \label{fig:angular-velocity-vector}%
\end{figure}

The kinetic energy of our rotating body is
\begin{subequations}
    \begin{align}
        T &= \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i} m_i \dot{\vb{r}}_i^2 \\
        &= \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i} m_i \pqty{\vb{r}_i \cross \vb*{\omega}}^2 \\
        &= \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i} m_i \pqty{\abs{\vb*{\omega}}^2 \abs{\vb{r}_i}^2 - \pqty{\vb*{\omega} \vdot \vb{r}_i}^2} \\
        &= \frac{1}{2} \omega_a I_{ab} \omega_b,
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}
where the \term{inertia tensor} \(\tens{I}\) is defined as
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:inertia-tensor}%
    I_{ab} = \sum_i m_i \pqty{\kronecker_{ab} \abs{\vb{r}_i}^2 - r_{ia} r_{ib}}.
\end{equation}

Note that since the locations \(\vb{r}_i\) of each particle are fixed with respect to the body frame, \(\tens{I}\) is constant. Furthermore, \(I_{ab} = I_{ba}\) is symmetric, and it is positive-definite: for any vector \(\vb{c}\), we have
\begin{equation}
    c_A I_{Ab} c_B = \sum_{i} m_i \pqty{\abs{\vb{r}_i}^2 \abs{\vb{c}}^2 - \pqty{\vb{r}_i \vdot \vb{c}}^2} = \sum_{i} m_i \abs{\vb{r}_i}^2 \abs{\vb{c}}^2 \pqty{1 - \cos^2 \theta_i} \geq 0,
\end{equation}
with equality if and only if all the masses lies along the \(\vb{c}\) direction.

Hence, \(\tens{I}\) can be diagonalised, and its eigenvalues \(I_1, I_2, I_3 > 0\). The basis of eigenvectors will be orthogonal and could be chosen to be right-handed orthonormal. This defines a \textit{preferred} body frame, called the \term{principal body axes}, and the eigenvalues are called the \term{principal moments of intertia} of the body.

For continuous bodies, we have
\begin{equation}
    I_{ab} = \int \dd[3]{\vb{x}} \rho\pqty{\vb{x}} \pqty{\kronecker_{ab} \abs{\vb{x}}^2 - x_a x_b},
\end{equation}
or in matrix notation,
\begin{equation}
    I = \int \dd[3]{\vb{x}} \rho\pqty{\vb{x}} \pmqty{y^2 + z^2 & -xy & -xz \\ -xy & x^2 + z^2 & -yz \\ -xz & -yz & x^2 + y^2}.
\end{equation}

\begin{example}[Uniform Disc]
    Consider a uniform thin disc lying in the plane \(z = 0\) of radius \(a\). Then its density is given by
    \begin{equation}
        \rho\pqty{\vb{x}} = \frac{M \diracdelta\pqty{z}}{\pi a^2},
    \end{equation}
    where \(\diracdelta\) is the Dirac delta function. By symmetry, the principle axes will lie along the \(z\) direction, and (any choice of) two orthogonal directions in the \(z = 0\) plane. (Alternatively, we can see by symmetry that the moment of inertia tensor is diagonal in the standard basis.)

    We have
    \begin{equation}
        I_1 = \int_{r < a} \dd[3]{\vb{x}} \frac{M}{\pi a^2} \diracdelta\pqty{z} y^2
    \end{equation}
    and this is also equal to
    \begin{equation}
        I_2 = \int_{r < a} \dd[3]{\vb{x}} \frac{M}{\pi a^2} \diracdelta\pqty{z} x^2
    \end{equation}
    by symmetry, whereas \(I_3\) is given by
    \begin{equation}
        I_3 = \int_{r < a} \dd[3]{\vb{x}} \frac{M}{\pi a^2} \diracdelta\pqty{z} \pqty{x^2 + y^2} = I_1 + I_2.
    \end{equation}

    This evaluates to
    \begin{subequations}
        \begin{align}
            I_3 &= \frac{M}{\pi a^2} \int_{r < a} \dd{\pqty{x, y}} \pqty{x^2 + y^2} \\
            &= \frac{2M}{a^2} \int_{0}^{a} \dd{r} r^3 \\
            &= \frac{M a^2}{2},
        \end{align}
    \end{subequations}
    and hence \(I_1 = I_2 = \flatfrac{M a^2}{4}\).
\end{example}

The \term{angular momentum} about the centre of mass of the body is given by
\begin{subequations}
    \begin{align}
        \vb{L} &= \sum_{i} m_i \vb{r}_i \cross \dot{\vb{r}}_i \\
        &= \sum_{i} m_i \vb{r}_i \cross \pqty{\vb*{\omega} \cross \vb{r}} \\
        &= \sum_{i} m_i \pqty{\abs{\vb{r}_i}^2 \vb*{\omega} - \pqty{\vb*{\omega} \vdot \vb{r}_i} \vb{r}_i},
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}
but by \eqref{eq:inertia-tensor}, this is simply
\begin{equation}
    \vb{L} = I \vb*{\omega}.
\end{equation}

Note that typically (i.e. if all the eigenvalues \(I_a\) are not all equal), \(\vb{L}\) and \(\vb*{\omega}\) do \emph{not} necessarily point in the same direction. This will be important to remember, since what is conserved is the \emph{angular momentum}, not the most obvious observed quantity \emph{angular velocity}.

\subsection{Euler's Equations}

For a body rotating freely with no applied torque about its centre of mass, we have \(\dv*{\vb{L}}{t} = \vb{0}\). We observe that in the body frame, we have
\begin{equation}
    \vb{L} = L_a \pqty{t} \vb{e}_a \pqty{t}
\end{equation}
and the \emph{components} of the angular momentum in the body frame \emph{cannot} be fixed, unless the body frame is constant. So by the product rule,
\begin{equation}
    \dot{\vb{L}} = \dot{L}_a \vb{e}_a + L_a \dot{\vb{e}}_a = \dot{L}_a \vb{e}_a + L_a \pqty{\vb*{\omega} \cross \vb{e}_a}.
\end{equation}

Writing \(L_a = L_{ab} \omega_b\) and \(\dot{I}_{ab} = 0\), working in the \emph{principal body frame}, we have \(L_a = I_a \omega_a\) (for \(a = 1, 2, 3\)) and hence
\begin{subequations}
    \label{eq:euler-equations}%
    \begin{align}
        I_1 \dot{\omega}_1 + \omega_2 \omega_3 \pqty{I_3 - I_2} &= 0, \\
        I_2 \dot{\omega}_2 + \omega_3 \omega_1 \pqty{I_1 - I_3} &= 0, \\
        I_3 \dot{\omega}_3 + \omega_1 \omega_2 \pqty{I_2 - I_1} &= 0.
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}

\eqref{eq:euler-equations} are called \term{Euler's equations}.

\subsection{Free Tops}

A uniform sphere, by symmetry, will have \(I_1 = I_2 = I_3\). Then Euler's equations simply say that \(\dot{\vb*{\omega}} = \vb{0}\). This is the most trivial case.

\subsubsection{Symmetric Top}

\begin{figure}[ht!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.4\textwidth]{fig/ellipsoid.png}
    \caption{Ellipsoid}
    \label{fig:ellipsoid}%
\end{figure}

The symmetric top is an object with \(I_1 = I_2 \neq I_3\), and we will discuss the example of an ellipsoid, as in \autoref{fig:ellipsoid}. Then Euler's equations reduce to
\begin{subequations}
    \begin{align}
        I_1 \dot{\omega}_1 &= \omega_2 \omega_3 \pqty{I_1 - I_3}, \\
        I_1 \dot{\omega}_2 &= \omega_3 \omega_1 \pqty{I_3 - I_1}, \\
        I_3 \dot{\omega}_3 &= 0.
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}

Then \(\omega_3\) is constant, and the body spins at a fixed rate around its axis of symmetry. Then \(\dot{\omega}_1 = \Omega \omega_2\), and \(\dot{\omega}_2 = - \Omega \omega_1\), where
\begin{equation}
    \Omega = \omega_3 = \frac{I_! - I_3}{I_1}.
\end{equation}

This solves to
\begin{equation}
    \pqty{\omega_1, \omega_2} = \omega_0 \pqty{\sin \Omega t, \cos \Omega t}.
\end{equation}

This means that in the body frame, \(\vb*{\omega}\) \term{precesses} around the \(\vb{e}_3\) axis, as shown in \autoref{fig:ellipsoid-precession}. The direction of precession depends on whether \(I_1 > I_3\) or \(I_1 < I_3\).

\subsubsection{Asymmetric Top}

If \(I_1, I_2, I_3\) are all distinct, then Euler's equations are very difficult to solve in general. Let's consider the special case where the angular velocity \(\vb*{\omega}\) is perfectly aligned with a principal body axis, say \(\vb*{\omega} = \Omega = \vb{e}_1\). Then
\begin{align*}
    \vb*{\omega} \pqty{t} = \Omega \vb{e}_1
\end{align*}
for constant \(\Omega\) solves Euler's equation. Now, consider a small perturbation, so
\begin{equation}
    \vb*{\omega} \pqty{t} = \Omega \vb{e}_1 + \vb*{\eta}\pqty{t},
\end{equation}
where \(\abs{\vb*{\eta}} \ll \Omega\). Then expanding to linear order in \(\vb*{\eta}\), Euler's equations become
\begin{subequations}
    \begin{align}
        I_1 \dot{\eta}_1 &= 0, \\
        I_2 \dot{\eta}_2 &= \Omega \eta_3 \pqty{I_3 - I_1}, \\
        I_3 \dot{\eta}_3 &= \Omega \eta_2 \pqty{I_1 - I_2}.
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}

So differentiating a second time, we have for \(\eta_2\) that
\begin{equation}
    I_2 \ddot{\eta}_2 = \frac{\Omega^2}{I_3} \pqty{I_3 - I_1} \pqty{I_1 - I_2} \eta_2 = A \eta_2,
\end{equation}
for \(A = \frac{\Omega^2}{I_3} \pqty{I_3 - I_1} \pqty{I_1 - I_2}\).

Therefore, whether our perturbation remains small depends on the sign of \(A\). The motion is unstable if: \(I_3 > I_1 > I_2\) or \(I_2 > I_1 > I_3\), but otherwise stable.

In other words, rotation about the principal body axes with the \term{greatest} or \term{least} moment of inertia is stable, whereas rotation around the \term{intermediate} axis is unstable.

\begin{example}
    This is sometimes called the \term{tennis racket effect}, or the \term{Dzhanibekov effect}. Consider a table tennis bat. We expect the principle body axis to be one normal to the plane, one along the handle, and one along the width of the bat.

    The one with the greatest moment of inertia is the one normal to the plane, since the mass is distributed farthest from this axis. The one with the least moment of inertia is the one along the handle, since the mass is concentrated near this axis. The intermediate axis is along the width of the bat.

    As illustrated in \autoref{fig:tennis-racket}, we have \(I_3 > I_1 > I_2\). If we try to spin the bat around the intermediate axis, it will be unstable, and the bat will flip over.
\end{example}

\subsubsection{The Poinsot Construction}

But we do not know what happens as the perturbation that starts to grow, since the approximation simply breaks down. But what we know is, that as the asymmetric top rotates, both its energy \(E\) and the total angular momentum \(\vb{L}^2\) are conserved, so \(\vb*{\omega}\) must lie on the intersection on two ellipsoids:
\begin{subequations}
    \begin{align}
        E &= \frac{1}{2} \vb*{\omega} I \vb*{\omega} = \frac{1}{2} \pqty{I_1 \omega_1^2 + I_2 \omega_2^2 + I_3 \omega_3^2}, \\
        \vb{L}^2 &= \pqty{I \vb*{\omega}}^2 = I_1^2 \omega_1^2 + I_2^2 \omega_2^2 + I_3^2 \omega_3^2.
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}

Without loss of generality, we can take \(I_1 > I_2 > I_3\). Then we have
\begin{equation}
    \pqty{\frac{I_1}{I_2}}^2 > \frac{I_1}{I_2} > 1 > \frac{I_3}{I_2} > \pqty{\frac{I_3}{I_2}}^2.
\end{equation}

We sketch the surface of constant energy, known as the \term{inertial ellipsoid}. In the same space, we sketch the surface of constant angular momentum, known as the \term{angular momentum ellipsoid}. The angular momentum ellipsoid is more ``stretched`` in the \(\vb{e}_3\) direction.

We should imagine the angular momentum ellipsoid ``getting bigger'' and hence the first intersection of the two ellipsoids will initially be two points. After this, it will intersect at a pair of closed curves like circles.

On the opposite side, if we shrink the angular momentum ellipsoid, then the first intersection will be two points on the \(\vb{e}_1\) axis, and then it will intersect at a pair of closed curves like circles.

Interpolate between the solutions, we get \autoref{fig:inertial-ellipsoids}. Somewhere between the two behaviours, we get a separation curve, which is known as the \term{separatrix}.

\begin{figure}[ht!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{fig/inertial-ellipsoids.png}
    \caption{Inertial and Angular Momentum Ellipsoids}
    \label{fig:inertial-ellipsoids}%
\end{figure}

In the principle body frame, \(\vb*{\omega}\pqty{t}\) traces out the curve forming the intersection of these ellipoids for given \(\pqty{E, \vb{L}^2}\). This curves is called the \term{polhode}. (This also leads to the stability analysis conducted above.) This curve, and the two ellipsoids, are fixed in the \emph{body frame}. In the space frame, consider the following.

Since
\begin{equation}
    2E = \vb{\omega} \vdot \pqty{I \vb{\omega}} = \omega\pqty{t} \vdot \vb{L},
\end{equation}
we see that \(\vb*{\omega}\) lies in a plane, called the \term{invariable plane}, normal to \(\vb{L}\). Also, since \(\vb{L} = \grad_{\vb*{\omega}} E\), we see that the surface of constant energy is perpendicular to \(\vb{L}\), and hence tangent to the invariable plane. But the trace of the contact point of the invariable plane on the ellipsoid is the polhode, which is where the angular velocity vector \(\vb*{\omega}\) is located. The trace of the contact point on the invariable plane is called the \term{herpolhode}. This is shown in \autoref{fig:poinsot-construction}.

\subsection{Euler Angles}

So far, we have considered \(\vb*{\omega}\) in the body frame, but we have not parametrised the actual rotation matrix \(\vb{e}_a\pqty{t} = R_{ab}\pqty{t} \tilde{\vb{e}}_b\). \term{Euler angles} are a convenient parametrisation. It works in three stages.
