\section{Lagrangian Formulation}
\subsection{The Principle of Least Action}

We define the \term{action} of a system to be
\begin{equation}
    S\bqty{\vb{x}_i} = \int_{t_0}^{t_1} L \pqty{\vb{x}_i \pqty{t}, \dot{\vb{x}}_i \pqty{t}, t} \dd{t},
\end{equation}
where \(L\) is the \term{Lagrangian} of the system. Typically, the Lagrangian is given by
\begin{equation}
    L = T - V = \sum_{i = 1}^{n} \frac{1}{2} m_i \dot{\vb{x}}_i^2 - V\pqty{\Bqty{\vb{x}_j}_{j = 1}^{n}, t}.
\end{equation}

The \term{principle of least action} says if we consider all possible (smooth) paths (as in \autoref{fig:possible-paths}) with fixed endpoints \(\vb{x}_i \pqty{t_0}\) and \(\vb{x}_i \pqty{t_1}\), the true motion corresponds to the path that extremises the action \(S \bqty{\vb{x}_i}\),.

\begin{figure}[ht!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{fig/paths-of-particle.png}
    \caption{Possible Paths of a Particle}
    \label{fig:possible-paths}
\end{figure}

To see this, considering varying our path
\begin{equation}
    \vb{x}_i \pqty{t} \mapsto \vb{x}_i \pqty{t} + \var{\vb{x}_i \pqty{t}}
\end{equation}
with \(\var{\vb{x}_i} \pqty{t_0} = \var{\vb{x}_i} \pqty{t_1} = 0\). Then, we have
\begin{subequations}
    \begin{align}
        \var{S} &= S \bqty{\vb{x}_i + \var{\vb{x}_i}} - S \bqty{\vb{x}_i} \\
        &= \sum_{i = 1}^{n} \int_{t_0}^{t_1} \pqty{m \dot{\vb{x}}_i \vdot \var{\dot{\vb{x}}_i} -  \var{\vb{x}_i} \vdot \pdv{V}{\vb{x}_i}} \dd{t} + \bigO\pqty{\var{\vb{x}_i}^2} \\
        &= - \sum_{i = 1}^{n} \int_{t_0}^{t_1}\pqty{m_i \ddot{\vb{x}}_i + \pdv{V}{\vb{x}_i}} \vdot \var{\vb{x}_i} \dd{t} + \rightbar{\sum_{i = 1}^{n} m_i \vb{x}_i \vdot \var{\vb{x}_i}}_{t_0}^{t_1} +\bigO\pqty{\var{\vb{x}_i}^2} \\
        &= - \sum_{i = 1}^{n} \int_{t_0}^{t_1}\pqty{m_i \ddot{\vb{x}}_i + \pdv{V}{\vb{x}_i}} \vdot \var{\vb{x}_i} \dd{t} + +\bigO\pqty{\var{\vb{x}_i}^2}.
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}

For an extremum, we need \(\var{S} = 0\) for any such \(\var{\vb{x}_i}\pqty{t}\), which implies that \(m \ddot{\vb{x}}_i = - \pdv{V}{\vb{x}_i}\), reducing to Newton's second law.

We could not worry about \(L = T - V\) for now (just consider a general Lagrangian), and we consider a variation of \(\vb{x} \mapsto \vb{x} + \var{\vb{x}}\) and \(\dot{\vb{x}} \mapsto \dot{\vb{x}} + \var{\dot{\vb{x}}}\), giving
\begin{subequations}
    \begin{align}
        \var{S} &= \int_{t_0}^{t_1} L \pqty{\vb{x} + \var{\vb{x}}, \dot{\vb{x}} + \var{\dot{\vb{x}}}, t} \dd{t} - \int_{t_0}^{t_1} L \pqty{\vb{x}, \dot{\vb{x}}, t} \dd{t} \\
        &= \int_{t_0}^{t_1} \pqty{\pdv{L}{x^a} \vdot \var{x^a} + \pdv{L}{\dot{x}^a} \vdot \var{\dot{x}^a}} \dd{t} + \bigO\pqty{\var{\vb{x}}^2} &&  \var{\dot{x}^a} = \dv{t} \var{x^a} \\
        &= \int_{t_0}^{t_1} \bqty{\pdv{L}{x^a} - \dv{t} \pdv{L}{\dot{x}^a}} \var{x^a} \dd{t} + \rightbar{\pdv{L}{\dot{x}^a} \var{x}^a}_{t_0}^{t_1} + \bigO\pqty{\var{\vb{x}}^2}.
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}
where \(a = 1, 2, \ldots, 3n\) are for \(n\) particles in \(\RR^3\). Note the boundary terms vanish since \(\var{\vb{x}}\pqty{t_0} = \var{\vb{x}}\pqty{t_1} = 0\).

For an extremum, we need \(\var{S} = 0\) for any such \(\var{\vb{x}}\), which leads to the \term{Euler--Lagrange equations}
\begin{equation}
    \dv{t} \pqty{\pdv{L}{\dot{x}^a}} - \pdv{L}{x^a} = 0.
\end{equation}

\begin{remarks}
    \begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
        \item The choice of the Lagrangian is not unique. Consider the new Lagrangian
        \begin{equation}
            L' \pqty{\vb{x}, \dot{\vb{x}}, t} = L \pqty{\vb{x}, \dot{\vb{x}}, t} + \dv{t} f\pqty{\vb{x}, t}
        \end{equation}
        where note \(f\) does not depend on \(\dot{\vb{x}}\). Then
        \begin{equation}
            S' = \int L' \dd{t} = S + \rightbar{f\pqty{x, t}}_{t_0}^{t_1}
        \end{equation}
        and the boundary term does not change as we vary our motion with fixed endpoints.

        \begin{figure}[ht!]
            \centering
            \includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{fig/simple-harmonic-oscillator.png}
            \caption{Simple Harmonic Oscillator}
            \label{fig:simple-harmonic-oscillator}
        \end{figure}

        \item The true motion extremises the option, but does not necessarily \emph{minimise} it. In particular, it could be a saddle point. Consider a one-dimensional simple harmonic oscillator as given in \autoref{fig:simple-harmonic-oscillator}. The Lagrangian in this case is given by
        \begin{equation}
            L = \frac{1}{2} m \dot{x}^2 - \frac{1}{2} m \omega^2 x^2.
        \end{equation}

        Consider the trivial solution \(x\pqty{t} = 0\) for all \(t\), and alternatively, the motion given by
        \begin{equation}
            x_\epsilon \pqty{t} = \epsilon \sin \pqty{\frac{\pi \pqty{t - t_0}}{t_1 - t_0}}
        \end{equation}
        where \(S = \int_{t_0}^{t_1} L \dd{t}\).

        The path \(x_\epsilon \pqty{t}\) is \emph{not} claimed to be a solution, but it does obey \(x_\epsilon\pqty{t_0} = x_\epsilon\pqty{t_1} = 0\).

        We have
        \begin{equation}
            \dot{x}^2 = \pqty{\frac{\pi}{t_1 - t_0}}^2 \epsilon^2 \cos^2 \pqty{\frac{\pi \pqty{t - t_0}}{t_1 - t_0}}
        \end{equation}
        so
        \begin{subequations}
            \begin{align}
                S\bqty{x_\epsilon} &= \frac{1}{2} m \int_{t_0}^{t_1} \bqty{\pqty{\frac{\pi}{t_1 - t_0}}^2 \epsilon^2 \cos^2 \pqty{\frac{\pi \pqty{t - t_0}}{t_1 - t_0}} - \omega^2 \epsilon^2 \sin^2 \pqty{\frac{\pi \pqty{t - t_0}}{t_1 - t_0}}} \dd{t} \\
                &= \frac{m\epsilon^2}{4} \bqty{\pqty{\frac{\pi}{t_1 - t_0}}^2 - \omega^2} \int_{t_0}^{t_1} \bqty{1 + \cos\pqty{\frac{2\pi\pqty{t - t_0}}{t_1 - t_0}}} \dd{t} \\
                &= \frac{m\epsilon^2}{4} \bqty{\pqty{\frac{\pi}{t_1 - t_0}}^2 - \omega^2} (t_1 - t_0)
            \end{align}
        \end{subequations}
        which could be any real number, depending on the value of \(\omega\) and \(t_1 - t_0\). This concludes that \(x_b \pqty{t} = 0\) was not a minimum, but rather a saddle point.

        \item When we solving for Newton's Second Law, we usually specify the initial location \(\vb{x} \pqty{0}\) and \(\dot{\vb{x}} \pqty{0}\), whereas in obtaining the Euler--Lagrange equations, we instead specified an initial location \(\vb{x} \pqty{0}\) and a final location \(\vb{x} \pqty{t_{\text{f}}}\). This does \emph{not} always give a unique solution. For example, if we recall from \textit{IB Variational Principles} about geodesics on a sphere being great circles, when we specify two points which are directly opposite each other on a sphere, there are infinitely many great circles connecting them, as shown in \autoref{fig:geodesics-on-sphere}.
        
        \begin{figure}[ht!]
            \centering
            \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{fig/geodesics-on-sphere.png}
            \caption{Geodesics on a Sphere}
            \label{fig:geodesics-on-sphere}
        \end{figure}
        
        \item The action \(S\) has dimensions of \(\bqty{S} = \dimM \dimL^2 \dimT^{-2}\), which is the same as \(\hbar\), so \(\flatfrac{S}{\hbar}\) is dimensionless.
        
        In Feynmann's approach to quantum mechanics, we consider all possible paths from \(\vb{x}_0\) to \(\vb{x}_1\), we conduct a ``path integral'' integrating over the space of \emph{all} possible paths, in the form
        \[
            \bra{\vb{x}_1, t_1} \ket{\vb{x}_0, t_0} = \int \mathcal{D}\vb{x} \exp\pqty{\frac{i}{\hbar} S\bqty{\vb{x}}}
        \]
        weighted by the action \(S\). Since \(\hbar \ll 1\) is very small, the path integral is dominated by the classical path that extremises the action, and in the limit \(\hbar \to 0\) is exactly what we have derived here.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{remarks}

\subsection{Generalised Coordinates}

An advantage of the Lagrangian formulation is that we can describe our motion using whatever coordinates we like. Suppose the coordinate transformation is given by
\begin{equation}
    x^a = x^a \pqty{q^b, t}
\end{equation}
for some other coordinates \(q^a\), known as \term{generalised coordinates} (for example, polar coordinates given by \(x = r \cos \theta\) and \(y = r \sin \theta\)). Then
\begin{subequations}
    \begin{align}
        L\pqty{x\pqty{t}, \dot{x}\pqty{t}, t} &= L\pqty{x\pqty{q, t}, \dot{x}\pqty{q, \dot{q}, t}, t} \\
        &= \tilde{L}\pqty{q\pqty{t}, \dot{q}\pqty{t}, t}
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}

By the partial derivatives chain rule,
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:xdot-chain-rule-in-qdot}%
    \dot{x}^b = \pdv{x^b}{q^c} \dot{q}^c + \pdv{x^b}{t}
\end{equation}
and hence
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:xdot-qdot-partial}%
    \rightbar{\pdv{\dot{x}^b}{\dot{q}^a}}_{t} = \pdv{x^b}{q^a}.
\end{equation}

We have
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:x-q-partial-time}%
    \dv{t} \pqty{\pdv{x^b}{q^a}} = \pdv{x^b}{q^d}{q^a} \dot{q}^d + \pdv{x^b}{t}{q^a}.
\end{equation}

Notice also from \autoref{eq:xdot-chain-rule-in-qdot} that
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:xdot-q-partial}%
    \pdv{\dot{x}^b}{q^a} = \pdv{q^a} \pqty{\pdv{x^b}{q^c} \dot{q}^c + \pdv{x^b}{t}} = \pdv{x^b}{q^a}{q^c} \dot{q}^c + \pdv{x^b}{q^a}{t},
\end{equation}
and since mixed partials commute, from \autoref{eq:x-q-partial-time} and \autoref{eq:xdot-q-partial}, we have
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:xdot-q-partial-x-q-partial-time-equality}%
    \dv{t} \pqty{\pdv{x^b}{q^a}} - \pdv{\dot{x}^b}{q^a} = 0.
\end{equation}

Now, consider using \(\tilde{L}\) and writing the Euler--Lagrange equations from varying \(q^a \pqty{t}\), we have
\begin{subequations}
    \begin{align}
        0 &= \dv{t} \pqty{\pdv{\tilde{L}}{\dot{q}^a}} - \pdv{\tilde{L}}{q^a} \\
        &= \dv{T} \pdv{L\pqty{x\pqty{q, t}, \dot{x}\pqty{q, \dot{q}, t}, t}}{\dot{q}^a} - \pdv{L\pqty{x\pqty{q, t}, \dot{x}\pqty{q, \dot{q}, t}, t}}{q^a} \\
        &= \dv{t} \pqty{\pdv{L}{\dot{x}^b} \pdv{\dot{x}^b}{\dot{q}^a}} - \pqty{\pdv{L}{x^b} \pdv{x^b}{q^a} + \pdv{L}{\dot{x}^b} \pdv{\dot{x}^b}{q^a}} \\
        &= \bqty{\dv{t} \pqty{\pdv{L}{\dot{x}^b}} - \pdv{L}{x^b}} \pdv{x^b}{q^a} + \pdv{L}{\dot{x}^b} \pqty{\dv{t} \pqty{\pdv{x^b}{q^a}} - \pdv{\dot{x}^b}{q^a}} && \text{by \autoref{eq:xdot-qdot-partial}} \\
        &= \bqty{\dv{t} \pqty{\pdv{L}{\dot{x}^b}} - \pdv{L}{x^b}} \pdv{x^b}{q^a}. && \text{by \autoref{eq:xdot-q-partial-x-q-partial-time-equality}}
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}

So provided the matrix \(\pdv*{x^b}{q^a}\) is invertible, for all \(t\), i.e., change of coordinates is invertible, the Euler--Lagrange equations in \(q^a\) are equivalent to the Euler--Lagrange equations in \(x^b\), i.e.,
\begin{equation}
    \dv{t} \pqty{\pdv{L}{\dot{x}^a}} - \pdv{L}{x^a} = 0 \iff \dv{t} \pqty{\pdv{\tilde{L}}{\dot{q}^a}} - \pdv{\tilde{L}}{q^a} = 0.
\end{equation}

For the case where the Lagrangian is given by \(L = T - V\), we have
\begin{equation}
    \pdv{L}{\dot{\vb{x}}} = m \dot{\vb{x}} = \vb{p}
\end{equation}
which is the standard momentum. For the generalised coordinates, we define the partial derivatives
\begin{equation}
    \pdv{\tilde{L}}{\dot{q}^a} = p_a
\end{equation}
which is the \term{generalised momentum} by analogy.

\begin{example}
    Suppose a particle has motion \(\vb{r} = \vb{r}\pqty{t}\) in some inertial frame, and suppose another frame rotates with angular velocity \(\vb*{\omega} = \vb*{\omega} \pqty{t}\) with respect to our inertial frame. Then, in the rotating frame, the particle's velocity is described by 
    \begin{equation}
        \dot{\vb{r}} \pqty{t} = \dot{\vb{r}}' \pqty{t} + \vb*{\omega} \pqty{t} \cross \vb{r}' \pqty{t}.
    \end{equation}

    If this particle travels freely with constant potential \(V = 0\), then the Lagrangian will be
    \begin{equation}
        L = \frac{1}{2} m \dot{\vb{r}}^2 = \frac{1}{2} m \pqty{\dot{\vb{r}}' + \vb*{\omega} \cross \vb{r}'}^2 = \tilde{L} \pqty{\vb{r}', \dot{\vb{r}}', t}.
    \end{equation}

    Hence, the Euler--Lagrange equations for \(\tilde{L}\) are given by
    \begin{subequations}
        \begin{align}
            0 &= \dv{t} \pqty{\pdv{\tilde{L}\pqty{\dot{\vb{r}}'}}} - \pdv{L}{\vb{r}'} \\
            &= \dv{t} \pqty{m \pqty{\dot{\vb{r}}' + \vb*{\omega} \cross \vb{r}'}} - m \pqty{\dot{\vb{r}}' + \vb*{\omega} \cross \vb{r}'} \cross \vb*{\omega},
        \end{align}
    \end{subequations}
    i.e.,
    \begin{equation}
        \ddot{\vb{r}}' + 2 \vb*{\omega} \cross \dot{\vb{r}}' + \dot{\vb*{\omega}} \cross \vb{r}' + \vb*{\omega} \cross \pqty{\vb*{\omega} \cross \vb{r}'} = 0
    \end{equation}
    which recovers the \term{Coriolis force} \(2 m \vb*{\omega} \cross \dot{\vb{r}}'\), the \term{Euler force} \(m \dot{\vb*{\omega}} \cross \vb{r}'\), and the \term{centrifugal force} \(m \vb*{\omega} \cross \pqty{\vb*{\omega} \cross \vb{r}'}\) from \textit{IA Dynamics and Relativity}.
\end{example}

\subsection{Constraints and LAgrange Multipliers}

Suppose a system of \(n\) particles in \(\RR^3\) has degrees of freedom \(x^a \pqty{t}\) which are subject to constraints of the form
\begin{equation}
    f_A \pqty{x^a\pqty{t}, t} = 0, \quad A = 1, 2, \ldots, m.
\end{equation}

Such conditions where the constraints are equalities, and which satisfy
\begin{equation}
    \pdv{f_A}{\dot{x}^a} = 0
\end{equation}
are called \term{holonomic}.

The special case where the constraints have no explicit time dependence, i.e.,
\begin{equation}
    \pdv*{f_A}{t} = 0
\end{equation}
is called a \term{scleronomous} constraint, and otherwise   is called a \term{rheonomic} constraint.

We may enforce these constraints using \term{Lagrange multipliers}. We set
\begin{equation}
    L'\pqty{x^a, \dot{x}^a, \lambda^A, t} = L\pqty{x^a, \dot{x}^a, t} + \sum_{A = 1}^{k}\lambda^A \pqty{t} f_A \pqty{x^a, t}
\end{equation}
where \(L\) is the original Lagrangian for \emph{unconstrained} motion, and \(\lambda^A \pqty{t}\) are the Lagrange multipliers. Such Lagrange multipliers should be time-dependent, since we want the constraints to hold at \emph{all} times, rather than simply holding \emph{on average}.

We treat the set of Lagrange multipliers \(\Bqty{\lambda^A \pqty{t}}\) as the new coordinates for the system. The Euler--Lagrange equations for \(L'\) give
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:euler-lagrange-with-constraints}%
    \dv{t} \pqty{\pdv{L}{\dot{x}^a}} - \pdv{L}{x^a} = \sum_{A = 1}^{k} \lambda^A \pqty{t} \pdv{f_A}{x^a},
\end{equation}
for the original coordinates \(x^a\), and varying with respect to Lagrange multipliers, this simply recovers the constraints
\begin{equation}
    f_A \pqty{x^a, t} = 0.
\end{equation}

The left-hand side of \autoref{eq:euler-lagrange-with-constraints} is the same as the unconstrained Euler--Lagrange equations, and the right-hand side given by
\begin{equation}
    R_a = \sum_{A = 1}^{k} \lambda^A \pqty{t} \pdv{f_A}{x^a}
\end{equation}
is known as the \term{constraint force}.

\begin{figure}[ht!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{fig/constraint-surface.png}
    \caption{Constraint Surface}
    \label{fig:constraint-surface}
\end{figure}

If \(\var{x^a}\) is any variation tangent to the constraint surface, as sketched in \autoref{fig:constraint-force}, then the constraint force does no work, i.e.,
\begin{equation}
    \sum_{a = 1}^{3n} R_a \var{x^a} = 0.
\end{equation}

In other words, the equation
\begin{equation}
    R_a = \sum_{A = 1}^{k} \lambda^A \pqty{t} \pdv{f_A}{x^a}
\end{equation}
is normal to the constraint surface, and in a way, a force that we would have to overcome to move off the constraint surface.

\begin{figure}[ht!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.3\textwidth]{fig/pendulum.png}
    \caption{Constraint Motion of Pendulum}
    \label{fig:constraint-motion-of-pendulum}
\end{figure}

\begin{example}
    Consider a mass on a rod of fixed length \(l\) divided at one end and swinging under gravity, as demonstrated in \autoref{fig:constraint-motion-of-pendulum}, with the constrained Lagrangian therefore given by
    \begin{equation}
        L' = \frac{1}{2} m \pqty{\dot{x}^2 + \dot{y}^2} - m g y + \lambda \pqty{x^2 + y^2 - l^2}.
    \end{equation}

    We get
    \begin{subequations}
        \begin{align}
            m \ddot{x} &= 2 \lambda x \\
            m \ddot{y} &= - mg 2 \lambda y \\
            l^2 &= x^2 + y^2 
        \end{align}
    \end{subequations}
    and in this case, the constrained force is \(2 \lambda \pqty{x, y}\transpose\), where we identify \(\lambda \sim T\) is the tension in the rod.
\end{example}

It is often very useful to solve the constraints before computing the Euler--Lagrange equations. For example, suppose we have a system of coordinates \(q^b\), such that \(q^b\) for \(b = 1, 2, \ldots, 3b - k\) are coordinates along the constraint surface, while the remaining coordinates
\begin{equation}
    q^{3n - k + A} = f_A
\end{equation}
for \(A = 1, 2, \ldots, k\) are just the constraints themselves. For example, if we lie on a sphere, we might have chosen the standard Cartesian coordinates with constraint \(x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = l^2\). Instead, we could choose the natural spherical coordinates \(q^1 = \theta\), \(q^2 = \phi\) and \(q^3 = r - l\). Then, our constraint surface simply becomes \(q^3 = 0\), and \(\pqty{q^1, q^2} = \pqty{\theta, \phi}\) parametrises the surface of the sphere. In these coordinates, the Lagrangian will be given by
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{L}' \pqty{q, \dot{q}, \lambda, t} = \tilde{L} \pqty{q^a, \dot{q}^a, t} + \sum_{A = 1}^{k} \lambda^A \pqty{t} q^{3n - k + A}.
\end{equation} 

Imposing the constraint amounts to setting \(q^{3n - k + A} = 0\) for \(A = 1, 2, \ldots, k\), and the motion reduces to the motion along the surface, in particular,
\begin{equation}
    \dv{t} \pqty{\pdv{\tilde{L}}{\dot{q}^b}} - \pdv{\tilde{L}}{q^b} = \sum_{A = 1}^{k} \lambda^A \pqty{t} \pdv{q^{3n - k + A}}{q^b} = 0
\end{equation}
for \(b = 1, 2, \ldots, 3n - k\), and the coordinates along the constraint surface are free of the Lagrange multipliers, which is identical to simply the free, unconstrained motion in the first place.
