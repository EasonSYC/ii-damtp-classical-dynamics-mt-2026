\section{Lagrangian Formulation}
\subsection{The Principle of Least Action}

We define the \term{action} of a system to be
\begin{equation}
    S\bqty{\vb{x}_i} = \int_{t_0}^{t_1} L \pqty{\vb{x}_i \pqty{t}, \dot{\vb{x}}_i \pqty{t}, t} \dd{t},
\end{equation}
where \(L\) is the \term{Lagrangian} of the system. Typically, the Lagrangian is given by
\begin{equation}
    L = T - V = \sum_{i = 1}^{n} \frac{1}{2} m_i \dot{\vb{x}}_i^2 - V\pqty{\Bqty{\vb{x}_j}_{j = 1}^{n}, t}.
\end{equation}

The \term{principle of least action} says if we consider all possible (smooth) paths (as in \autoref{fig:possible-paths}) with fixed endpoints \(\vb{x}_i \pqty{t_0}\) and \(\vb{x}_i \pqty{t_1}\), the true motion corresponds to the path that extremises the action \(S \bqty{\vb{x}_i}\),.

\begin{figure}[ht!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{fig/paths-of-particle.png}
    \caption{Possible Paths of a Particle}
    \label{fig:possible-paths}
\end{figure}

To see this, considering varying our path
\begin{equation}
    \vb{x}_i \pqty{t} \mapsto \vb{x}_i \pqty{t} + \var{\vb{x}_i \pqty{t}}
\end{equation}
with \(\var{\vb{x}_i} \pqty{t_0} = \var{\vb{x}_i} \pqty{t_1} = 0\). Then, we have
\begin{subequations}
    \begin{align}
        \var{S} &= S \bqty{\vb{x}_i + \var{\vb{x}_i}} - S \bqty{\vb{x}_i} \\
        &= \sum_{i = 1}^{n} \int_{t_0}^{t_1} \pqty{m \dot{\vb{x}}_i \vdot \var{\dot{\vb{x}}_i} -  \var{\vb{x}_i} \vdot \pdv{V}{\vb{x}_i}} \dd{t} + \bigO\pqty{\var{\vb{x}_i}^2} \\
        &= - \sum_{i = 1}^{n} \int_{t_0}^{t_1}\pqty{m_i \ddot{\vb{x}}_i + \pdv{V}{\vb{x}_i}} \vdot \var{\vb{x}_i} \dd{t} + \rightbar{\sum_{i = 1}^{n} m_i \vb{x}_i \vdot \var{\vb{x}_i}}_{t_0}^{t_1} +\bigO\pqty{\var{\vb{x}_i}^2} \\
        &= - \sum_{i = 1}^{n} \int_{t_0}^{t_1}\pqty{m_i \ddot{\vb{x}}_i + \pdv{V}{\vb{x}_i}} \vdot \var{\vb{x}_i} \dd{t} + +\bigO\pqty{\var{\vb{x}_i}^2}.
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}

For an extremum, we need \(\var{S} = 0\) for any such \(\var{\vb{x}_i}\pqty{t}\), which implies that \(m \ddot{\vb{x}}_i = - \pdv{V}{\vb{x}_i}\), reducing to Newton's second law.

We could not worry about \(L = T - V\) for now (just consider a general Lagrangian), and we consider a variation of \(\vb{x} \mapsto \vb{x} + \var{\vb{x}}\) and \(\dot{\vb{x}} \mapsto \dot{\vb{x}} + \var{\dot{\vb{x}}}\), giving
\begin{subequations}
    \begin{align}
        \var{S} &= \int_{t_0}^{t_1} L \pqty{\vb{x} + \var{\vb{x}}, \dot{\vb{x}} + \var{\dot{\vb{x}}}, t} \dd{t} - \int_{t_0}^{t_1} L \pqty{\vb{x}, \dot{\vb{x}}, t} \dd{t} \\
        &= \int_{t_0}^{t_1} \pqty{\pdv{L}{x^a} \vdot \var{x^a} + \pdv{L}{\dot{x}^a} \vdot \var{\dot{x}^a}} \dd{t} + \bigO\pqty{\var{\vb{x}}^2} &&  \var{\dot{x}^a} = \dv{t} \var{x^a} \\
        &= \int_{t_0}^{t_1} \bqty{\pdv{L}{x^a} - \dv{t} \pdv{L}{\dot{x}^a}} \var{x^a} \dd{t} + \rightbar{\pdv{L}{\dot{x}^a} \var{x}^a}_{t_0}^{t_1} + \bigO\pqty{\var{\vb{x}}^2}.
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}
where \(a = 1, 2, \ldots, 3n\) are for \(n\) particles in \(\RR^3\). Note the boundary terms vanish since \(\var{\vb{x}}\pqty{t_0} = \var{\vb{x}}\pqty{t_1} = 0\).

For an extremum, we need \(\var{S} = 0\) for any such \(\var{\vb{x}}\), which leads to the \term{Euler--Lagrange equations}
\begin{equation}
    \dv{t} \pqty{\pdv{L}{\dot{x}^a}} - \pdv{L}{x^a} = 0.
\end{equation}

\begin{remarks}
    \begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
        \item The choice of the Lagrangian is not unique. Consider the new Lagrangian
        \begin{equation}
            L' \pqty{\vb{x}, \dot{\vb{x}}, t} = L \pqty{\vb{x}, \dot{\vb{x}}, t} + \dv{t} f\pqty{\vb{x}, t}
        \end{equation}
        where note \(f\) does not depend on \(\dot{\vb{x}}\). Then
        \begin{equation}
            S' = \int L' \dd{t} = S + \rightbar{f\pqty{x, t}}_{t_0}^{t_1}
        \end{equation}
        and the boundary term does not change as we vary our motion with fixed endpoints.

        \begin{figure}[ht!]
            \centering
            \includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{fig/simple-harmonic-oscillator.png}
            \caption{Simple Harmonic Oscillator}
            \label{fig:simple-harmonic-oscillator}
        \end{figure}

        \item The true motion extremises the option, but does not necessarily \emph{minimise} it. In particular, it could be a saddle point. Consider a one-dimensional simple harmonic oscillator as given in \autoref{fig:simple-harmonic-oscillator}. The Lagrangian in this case is given by
        \begin{equation}
            L = \frac{1}{2} m \dot{x}^2 - \frac{1}{2} m \omega^2 x^2.
        \end{equation}

        Consider the trivial solution \(x\pqty{t} = 0\) for all \(t\), and alternatively, the motion given by
        \begin{equation}
            x_\epsilon \pqty{t} = \epsilon \sin \pqty{\frac{\pi \pqty{t - t_0}}{t_1 - t_0}}
        \end{equation}
        where \(S = \int_{t_0}^{t_1} L \dd{t}\).

        The path \(x_\epsilon \pqty{t}\) is \emph{not} claimed to be a solution, but it does obey \(x_\epsilon\pqty{t_0} = x_\epsilon\pqty{t_1} = 0\).

        We have
        \begin{equation}
            \dot{x}^2 = \pqty{\frac{\pi}{t_1 - t_0}}^2 \epsilon^2 \cos^2 \pqty{\frac{\pi \pqty{t - t_0}}{t_1 - t_0}}
        \end{equation}
        so
        \begin{subequations}
            \begin{align}
                S\bqty{x_\epsilon} &= \frac{1}{2} m \int_{t_0}^{t_1} \bqty{\pqty{\frac{\pi}{t_1 - t_0}}^2 \epsilon^2 \cos^2 \pqty{\frac{\pi \pqty{t - t_0}}{t_1 - t_0}} - \omega^2 \epsilon^2 \sin^2 \pqty{\frac{\pi \pqty{t - t_0}}{t_1 - t_0}}} \dd{t} \\
                &= \frac{m\epsilon^2}{4} \bqty{\pqty{\frac{\pi}{t_1 - t_0}}^2 - \omega^2} \int_{t_0}^{t_1} \bqty{1 + \cos\pqty{\frac{2\pi\pqty{t - t_0}}{t_1 - t_0}}} \dd{t} \\
                &= \frac{m\epsilon^2}{4} \bqty{\pqty{\frac{\pi}{t_1 - t_0}}^2 - \omega^2} (t_1 - t_0)
            \end{align}
        \end{subequations}
        which could be any real number, depending on the value of \(\omega\) and \(t_1 - t_0\). This concludes that \(x_b \pqty{t} = 0\) was not a minimum, but rather a saddle point.

        \item When we solving for Newton's Second Law, we usually specify the initial location \(\vb{x} \pqty{0}\) and \(\dot{\vb{x}} \pqty{0}\), whereas in obtaining the Euler--Lagrange equations, we instead specified an initial location \(\vb{x} \pqty{0}\) and a final location \(\vb{x} \pqty{t_{\text{f}}}\). This does \emph{not} always give a unique solution. For example, if we recall from \textit{IB Variational Principles} about geodesics on a sphere being great circles, when we specify two points which are directly opposite each other on a sphere, there are infinitely many great circles connecting them, as shown in \autoref{fig:geodesics-on-sphere}.
        
        \begin{figure}[ht!]
            \centering
            \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{fig/geodesics-on-sphere.png}
            \caption{Geodesics on a Sphere}
            \label{fig:geodesics-on-sphere}
        \end{figure}
        
        \item The action \(S\) has dimensions of \(\bqty{S} = \dimM \dimL^2 \dimT^{-2}\), which is the same as \(\hbar\), so \(\flatfrac{S}{\hbar}\) is dimensionless.
        
        In Feynmann's approach to quantum mechanics, we consider all possible paths from \(\vb{x}_0\) to \(\vb{x}_1\), we conduct a ``path integral'' integrating over the space of \emph{all} possible paths, in the form
        \[
            \bra{\vb{x}_1, t_1} \ket{\vb{x}_0, t_0} = \int \mathcal{D}\vb{x} \exp\pqty{\frac{i}{\hbar} S\bqty{\vb{x}}}
        \]
        weighted by the action \(S\). Since \(\hbar \ll 1\) is very small, the path integral is dominated by the classical path that extremises the action, and in the limit \(\hbar \to 0\) is exactly what we have derived here.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{remarks}

\subsection{Generalised Coordinates}

An advantage of the Lagrangian formulation is that we can describe our motion using whatever coordinates we like. Suppose the coordinate transformation is given by
\begin{equation}
    x^a = x^a \pqty{q^b, t}
\end{equation}
for some other coordinates \(q^a\), known as \term{generalised coordinates} (for example, polar coordinates given by \(x = r \cos \theta\) and \(y = r \sin \theta\)). Then
\begin{subequations}
    \begin{align}
        L\pqty{x\pqty{t}, \dot{x}\pqty{t}, t} &= L\pqty{x\pqty{q, t}, \dot{x}\pqty{q, \dot{q}, t}, t} \\
        &= \tilde{L}\pqty{q\pqty{t}, \dot{q}\pqty{t}, t}
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}

By the partial derivatives chain rule,
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:xdot-chain-rule-in-qdot}%
    \dot{x}^b = \pdv{x^b}{q^c} \dot{q}^c + \pdv{x^b}{t}
\end{equation}
and hence
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:xdot-qdot-partial}%
    \rightbar{\pdv{\dot{x}^b}{\dot{q}^a}}_{t} = \pdv{x^b}{q^a}.
\end{equation}

We have
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:x-q-partial-time}%
    \dv{t} \pqty{\pdv{x^b}{q^a}} = \pdv{x^b}{q^d}{q^a} \dot{q}^d + \pdv{x^b}{t}{q^a}.
\end{equation}

Notice also from \autoref{eq:xdot-chain-rule-in-qdot} that
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:xdot-q-partial}%
    \pdv{\dot{x}^b}{q^a} = \pdv{q^a} \pqty{\pdv{x^b}{q^c} \dot{q}^c + \pdv{x^b}{t}} = \pdv{x^b}{q^a}{q^c} \dot{q}^c + \pdv{x^b}{q^a}{t},
\end{equation}
and since mixed partials commute, from \autoref{eq:x-q-partial-time} and \autoref{eq:xdot-q-partial}, we have
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:xdot-q-partial-x-q-partial-time-equality}%
    \dv{t} \pqty{\pdv{x^b}{q^a}} - \pdv{\dot{x}^b}{q^a} = 0.
\end{equation}

Now, consider using \(\tilde{L}\) and writing the Euler--Lagrange equations from varying \(q^a \pqty{t}\), we have
\begin{subequations}
    \begin{align}
        0 &= \dv{t} \pqty{\pdv{\tilde{L}}{\dot{q}^a}} - \pdv{\tilde{L}}{q^a} \\
        &= \dv{T} \pdv{L\pqty{x\pqty{q, t}, \dot{x}\pqty{q, \dot{q}, t}, t}}{\dot{q}^a} - \pdv{L\pqty{x\pqty{q, t}, \dot{x}\pqty{q, \dot{q}, t}, t}}{q^a} \\
        &= \dv{t} \pqty{\pdv{L}{\dot{x}^b} \pdv{\dot{x}^b}{\dot{q}^a}} - \pqty{\pdv{L}{x^b} \pdv{x^b}{q^a} + \pdv{L}{\dot{x}^b} \pdv{\dot{x}^b}{q^a}} \\
        &= \bqty{\dv{t} \pqty{\pdv{L}{\dot{x}^b}} - \pdv{L}{x^b}} \pdv{x^b}{q^a} + \pdv{L}{\dot{x}^b} \pqty{\dv{t} \pqty{\pdv{x^b}{q^a}} - \pdv{\dot{x}^b}{q^a}} && \text{by \autoref{eq:xdot-qdot-partial}} \\
        &= \bqty{\dv{t} \pqty{\pdv{L}{\dot{x}^b}} - \pdv{L}{x^b}} \pdv{x^b}{q^a}. && \text{by \autoref{eq:xdot-q-partial-x-q-partial-time-equality}}
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}

So provided the matrix \(\pdv*{x^b}{q^a}\) is invertible, for all \(t\), i.e., change of coordinates is invertible, the Euler--Lagrange equations in \(q^a\) are equivalent to the Euler--Lagrange equations in \(x^b\), i.e.,
\begin{equation}
    \dv{t} \pqty{\pdv{L}{\dot{x}^a}} - \pdv{L}{x^a} = 0 \iff \dv{t} \pqty{\pdv{\tilde{L}}{\dot{q}^a}} - \pdv{\tilde{L}}{q^a} = 0.
\end{equation}

For the case where the Lagrangian is given by \(L = T - V\), we have
\begin{equation}
    \pdv{L}{\dot{\vb{x}}} = m \dot{\vb{x}} = \vb{p}
\end{equation}
which is the standard momentum. For the generalised coordinates, we define the partial derivatives
\begin{equation}
    \pdv{\tilde{L}}{\dot{q}^a} = p_a
\end{equation}
which is the \term{generalised momentum} by analogy.

\begin{example}
    Suppose a particle has motion \(\vb{r} = \vb{r}\pqty{t}\) in some inertial frame, and suppose another frame rotates with angular velocity \(\vb*{\omega} = \vb*{\omega} \pqty{t}\) with respect to our inertial frame. Then, in the rotating frame, the particle's velocity is described by 
    \begin{equation}
        \dot{\vb{r}} \pqty{t} = \dot{\vb{r}}' \pqty{t} + \vb*{\omega} \pqty{t} \cross \vb{r}' \pqty{t}.
    \end{equation}

    If this particle travels freely with constant potential \(V = 0\), then the Lagrangian will be
    \begin{equation}
        L = \frac{1}{2} m \dot{\vb{r}}^2 = \frac{1}{2} m \pqty{\dot{\vb{r}}' + \vb*{\omega} \cross \vb{r}'}^2 = \tilde{L} \pqty{\vb{r}', \dot{\vb{r}}', t}.
    \end{equation}

    Hence, the Euler--Lagrange equations for \(\tilde{L}\) are given by
    \begin{subequations}
        \begin{align}
            0 &= \dv{t} \pqty{\pdv{\tilde{L}\pqty{\dot{\vb{r}}'}}} - \pdv{L}{\vb{r}'} \\
            &= \dv{t} \pqty{m \pqty{\dot{\vb{r}}' + \vb*{\omega} \cross \vb{r}'}} - m \pqty{\dot{\vb{r}}' + \vb*{\omega} \cross \vb{r}'} \cross \vb*{\omega},
        \end{align}
    \end{subequations}
    i.e.,
    \begin{equation}
        \ddot{\vb{r}}' + 2 \vb*{\omega} \cross \dot{\vb{r}}' + \dot{\vb*{\omega}} \cross \vb{r}' + \vb*{\omega} \cross \pqty{\vb*{\omega} \cross \vb{r}'} = 0
    \end{equation}
    which recovers the \term{Coriolis force} \(2 m \vb*{\omega} \cross \dot{\vb{r}}'\), the \term{Euler force} \(m \dot{\vb*{\omega}} \cross \vb{r}'\), and the \term{centrifugal force} \(m \vb*{\omega} \cross \pqty{\vb*{\omega} \cross \vb{r}'}\) from \textit{IA Dynamics and Relativity}.
\end{example}

\subsection{Constraints and Lagrange Multipliers}

Suppose a system of \(n\) particles in \(\RR^3\) has degrees of freedom \(x^a \pqty{t}\) which are subject to constraints of the form
\begin{equation}
    f_A \pqty{x^a\pqty{t}, t} = 0, \quad A = 1, 2, \ldots, m.
\end{equation}

Such conditions where the constraints are equalities, and which satisfy
\begin{equation}
    \pdv{f_A}{\dot{x}^a} = 0
\end{equation}
are called \term{holonomic}.

The special case where the constraints have no explicit time dependence, i.e.,
\begin{equation}
    \pdv*{f_A}{t} = 0
\end{equation}
is called a \term{scleronomous} constraint, and otherwise   is called a \term{rheonomic} constraint.

We may enforce these constraints using \term{Lagrange multipliers}. We set
\begin{equation}
    L'\pqty{x^a, \dot{x}^a, \lambda^A, t} = L\pqty{x^a, \dot{x}^a, t} + \sum_{A = 1}^{k}\lambda^A \pqty{t} f_A \pqty{x^a, t}
\end{equation}
where \(L\) is the original Lagrangian for \emph{unconstrained} motion, and \(\lambda^A \pqty{t}\) are the Lagrange multipliers. Such Lagrange multipliers should be time-dependent, since we want the constraints to hold at \emph{all} times, rather than simply holding \emph{on average}.

We treat the set of Lagrange multipliers \(\Bqty{\lambda^A \pqty{t}}\) as the new coordinates for the system. The Euler--Lagrange equations for \(L'\) give
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:euler-lagrange-with-constraints}%
    \dv{t} \pqty{\pdv{L}{\dot{x}^a}} - \pdv{L}{x^a} = \sum_{A = 1}^{k} \lambda^A \pqty{t} \pdv{f_A}{x^a},
\end{equation}
for the original coordinates \(x^a\), and varying with respect to Lagrange multipliers, this simply recovers the constraints
\begin{equation}
    f_A \pqty{x^a, t} = 0.
\end{equation}

The left-hand side of \autoref{eq:euler-lagrange-with-constraints} is the same as the unconstrained Euler--Lagrange equations, and the right-hand side given by
\begin{equation}
    R_a = \sum_{A = 1}^{k} \lambda^A \pqty{t} \pdv{f_A}{x^a}
\end{equation}
is known as the \term{constraint force}.

\begin{figure}[ht!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{fig/constraint-surface.png}
    \caption{Constraint Surface}
    \label{fig:constraint-surface}
\end{figure}

If \(\var{x^a}\) is any variation tangent to the constraint surface, as sketched in \autoref{fig:constraint-force}, then the constraint force does no work, i.e.,
\begin{equation}
    \sum_{a = 1}^{3n} R_a \var{x^a} = 0.
\end{equation}

In other words, the equation
\begin{equation}
    R_a = \sum_{A = 1}^{k} \lambda^A \pqty{t} \pdv{f_A}{x^a}
\end{equation}
is normal to the constraint surface, and in a way, a force that we would have to overcome to move off the constraint surface.

\begin{figure}[ht!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.3\textwidth]{fig/pendulum.png}
    \caption{Constraint Motion of Pendulum}
    \label{fig:constraint-motion-of-pendulum}
\end{figure}

\begin{example}
    Consider a mass on a rod of fixed length \(l\) divided at one end and swinging under gravity, as demonstrated in \autoref{fig:constraint-motion-of-pendulum}, with the constrained Lagrangian therefore given by
    \begin{equation}
        L' = \frac{1}{2} m \pqty{\dot{x}^2 + \dot{y}^2} - m g y + \lambda \pqty{x^2 + y^2 - l^2}.
    \end{equation}

    We get
    \begin{subequations}
        \begin{align}
            m \ddot{x} &= 2 \lambda x \\
            m \ddot{y} &= - mg 2 \lambda y \\
            l^2 &= x^2 + y^2 
        \end{align}
    \end{subequations}
    and in this case, the constrained force is \(2 \lambda \pqty{x, y}\transpose\), where we identify \(\lambda \sim T\) is the tension in the rod.
\end{example}

It is often very useful to solve the constraints before computing the Euler--Lagrange equations. For example, suppose we have a system of coordinates \(q^b\), such that \(q^b\) for \(b = 1, 2, \ldots, 3b - k\) are coordinates along the constraint surface, while the remaining coordinates
\begin{equation}
    q^{3n - k + A} = f_A
\end{equation}
for \(A = 1, 2, \ldots, k\) are just the constraints themselves. For example, if we lie on a sphere, we might have chosen the standard Cartesian coordinates with constraint \(x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = l^2\). Instead, we could choose the natural spherical coordinates \(q^1 = \theta\), \(q^2 = \phi\) and \(q^3 = r - l\). Then, our constraint surface simply becomes \(q^3 = 0\), and \(\pqty{q^1, q^2} = \pqty{\theta, \phi}\) parametrises the surface of the sphere. In these coordinates, the Lagrangian will be given by
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{L}' \pqty{q, \dot{q}, \lambda, t} = \tilde{L} \pqty{q^a, \dot{q}^a, t} + \sum_{A = 1}^{k} \lambda^A \pqty{t} q^{3n - k + A}.
\end{equation} 

Imposing the constraint amounts to setting \(q^{3n - k + A} = 0\) for \(A = 1, 2, \ldots, k\), and the motion reduces to the motion along the surface, in particular,
\begin{equation}
    \dv{t} \pqty{\pdv{\tilde{L}}{\dot{q}^b}} - \pdv{\tilde{L}}{q^b} = \sum_{A = 1}^{k} \lambda^A \pqty{t} \pdv{q^{3n - k + A}}{q^b} = 0
\end{equation}
for \(b = 1, 2, \ldots, 3n - k\), and the coordinates along the constraint surface are free of the Lagrange multipliers, which is identical to simply the free, unconstrained motion in the first place.

Which of the two strategies above is simpler depends on the problem.

\begin{example}[Bead and Rotating Hoop]
    Consider the setup in \autoref{fig:bead-on-rotating-hoop}. A bead is sliding on a rotation hoop of radius \(a\), whose axis is aligned with the \(z\)-axis, and the plane of the hoop makes an angle \(\phi\) to the \(x\) axis, rotating with angular speed \(\omega\). The mass is free to move along the hoop, with an angle \(\psi\) from vertical (from negative \(z\)-direction).

    In this case we have two constraints.
    \begin{itemize}
        \item the bead lies on the hoop;
        \item the hoop rotates with frequency \(\omega\).
    \end{itemize}

    Here it's simplest to parametrise solutions to these constraints using \(\psi\pqty{t}\) as a generalised coordinate, as the only degree of freedom. Then we have
    \begin{subequations}
        \begin{align}
            x\pqty{t} &= a \sin \psi\pqty{t} \cos \pqty{\omega t} \\
            y\pqty{t} &= a \sin \psi\pqty{t} \sin \pqty{\omega t} \\
            z\pqty{t} &= a - a \cos \psi\pqty{t}
        \end{align}
    \end{subequations}
    
    The Lagrangian then becomes
    \begin{subequations}
        \begin{align}
            L &= \frac{1}{2} m \dot{\vb{x}}^2 - mg z \\
            &= ma^2 \pqty{\frac{1}{2} \dot{\psi}^2 - V_{\text{eff}}},
        \end{align}
    \end{subequations}
    where the effective potential is given by
    \begin{equation}
        V_{\text{eff}} \pqty{\psi} = \frac{1}{a} \pqty{- g \cos \psi - \frac{1}{2} a \omega^2 \sin^2 \psi}.
    \end{equation}

    Then the Euler--Lagrange equations give
    \begin{equation}
        0 = \dv{t} \pqty{\pdv{L}{\dot{\psi}}} - \pdv{L}{\psi}
    \end{equation}
    which gives
    \begin{equation}
        \ddot{\psi} = - \pdv{V_{\text{eff}}}{\psi}.
    \end{equation}

    Consider a solution of the form \(\dot{\psi} = 0\), so the bead sits at fixed angle in the \(z\)-axis. This solution must be at a critical point of the effective potential, i.e., \(\pdv*{V_{\text{eff}}}{\psi} = 0\), which gives
    \begin{equation}
        \sin \psi \pqty{g - a \omega^2 \cos \psi} = 0.
    \end{equation}

    If \(\sin \psi = 0\), then \(\psi = 0\) or \(\psi = \pi\), and if \(g - a \omega^2 \cos \psi = 0\), then \(\cos \psi = \flatfrac{g}{a \omega^2}\), provided \(\omega^2 \geq \flatfrac{g}{a}\).

    We consider the stability of these solutions by sketching the effective potential \(V_{\text{eff}}\) against \(\psi\), as in \autoref{fig:effective-potential-stability}. Observe that \(\psi = \pi\) is always unstable, while \(\psi = 0\) is stable when \(\omega^2 < \flatfrac{g}{a}\) and unstable when \(\omega^2 > \flatfrac{g}{a}\). The solution \(\cos \psi = \flatfrac{g}{a \omega^2}\) is stable whenever it exists.
\end{example}

\subsection{Noether's Theorem}

There is a close relation between \term{symmetries} of the Lagrangian and \term{conserved quantities}. We say some quantity \(F\pqty{q^a, \dot{q}^a, t}\) is \term{conserved} if
\begin{equation}
    \dv{F}{t} = \pdv{F}{q^a} \dot{q}^a + \pdv{F}{\dot{q}^a} \ddot{q}^a + \pdv{F}{t} = 0
\end{equation}
along the true trajectory, i.e. the solution to the Euler--Lagrange equations.

Consider continuously deforming a path \(q^a\pqty{t}\) to a path
\begin{equation}
    Q^a\pqty{t} = q^a\pqty{t} + \epsilon \eta^a\pqty{t} + \bigO\pqty{\epsilon^2}
\end{equation}
where \(\eta^a\pqty{t} = \eta^a\pqty{q\pqty{t}, \dot{q}\pqty{t}, t}\) in general, as illustrated in \autoref{fig:symmetry-of-lagrangian}. We say this generates a \term{symmetry} of \(L\), if
\begin{equation}
    \rightbar{\pdv{\epsilon} L\pqty{Q^a, \dot{Q}^a, t}}_{\epsilon = 0} = \dv{t} f\pqty{q, \dot{q}, t}
\end{equation}
for some function \(f\pqty{q, \dot{q}, t}\).

\begin{figure}[ht!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{fig/variation-of-path-symmetry.png}
    \caption{Deforming a Path and Symmetry of Lagrangian}
    \label{fig:symmetry-of-lagrangian}%
\end{figure}

\begin{remarks}
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item Very often, the function \(f\) is zero, and the Lagrangian is invariant. When \(f \neq 0\), our condition says the action \(S = \int_{t_0}^{t_1} L \dd{t}\) is changing only at the endpoints of the integral.
        \item To be a symmetry, this equation must hold for \term{all} paths \(q^a\pqty{t}\), whether or not this is a solution to the equations of motion.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{remarks}

\term{Noether's Theorem} states that if se have a continuous symmetry \(q^a \mapsto q^a + \epsilon \eta^a + \cdots\) of \(L\). Then the quantity given by
\begin{equation}
    F\pqty{q, \dot{q}, t} = \eta^a \pdv{L}{\dot{q}^a} - f
\end{equation}
is conserved, whenever the Euler--Lagrange equations hold.

\begin{proof}
    We have
    \begin{subequations}
        \begin{align}
            \rightbar{\pdv{t} L\pqty{q + \epsilon \eta, \dot{q} + \epsilon \dot{\eta}, t}}_{\epsilon = 0} &= \pdv{L}{q^a} \eta^a + \pdv{L}{\dot{q}^a} \dot{\eta}^a \\
            &= \dv{t} \pqty{\pdv{L}{\dot{q}^a}} \eta^a + \pdv{L}{\dot{q}^a} \dot{\eta}^a \\
            &= \dv{t} \pqty{\pdv{L}{\dot{q}^a} \eta^a}.
        \end{align}
    \end{subequations}

    Hence, if this deformation generates a symmetry, then
    \begin{equation}
        \dv{t} \pqty{\pdv{L}{\dot{q}^a} \eta^a - f} = 0,
    \end{equation}
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[Conservation of Momentum and Angular Momentum]
    Consider an \(n\)-particle system, with
    \begin{equation}
        L = \sum_{i = 1}^{n} \frac{1}{2} m_i \dot{\vb{x}}_i^2 - V\pqty{\abs{\vb{x}_i - \vb{x}_j}}.
    \end{equation}

    Under the deformation \(\vb{x}_i \pqty{t} \mapsto \vb{x}_i \pqty{t} + \epsilon \vb{a}\) for a constant \(\vb{a}\), we have simply \(\rightbar{\pdv*{L}{\epsilon}}_{\epsilon = 0} = 0\), so \(f = 0\). Then, by Noether's Theorem, we have a conserved quantity
    \begin{equation}
        \sum_{i = 1}^{n} \vb{a} \vdot \pdv{L}{\dot{\vb{x}}_i} = \vb{a} \vdot \sum_{i = 1}^{n} m_i \dot{\vb{x}}_i,
    \end{equation}
    i.e., the total momentum in the \(\vb{a}\) direction. But since \(\vb{a}\) is arbitrary, the total momentum \(\vb{P} = \sum_{i = 1}^{n} m_i \dot{\vb{x}}_i\) is conserved.

    In addition, consider rotating every particle infinitesimally about some axis \(\vb{n}\), so we have \(\vb{x}_i \mapsto \vb{x}_i + \epsilon \vb{n} \cross \vb{x}_i + \bigO\pqty{\epsilon^2}\) as in \autoref{fig:rotate-coordinates}. But since \(L\) is rotationally invariant, we have \(\rightbar{\pdv*{L}{\epsilon}}_{\epsilon = 0} = 0\), so \(f = 0\). Then, by Noether's Theorem, we have a conserved quantity
    \begin{equation}
        F = \sum_{i = 1}^{n} \pqty{\vb{n} \cross \vb{x}_i} \vdot \pdv{L}{\dot{\vb{x}}_i} = \sum_{i = 1}^{n} \pqty{\vb{n} \cross \vb{x}_i} \vdot m_i \dot{\vb{x}}_i = \vb{n} \vdot \pqty{\sum_{i = 1}^{n} m_i \vb{x}_i \cross \dot{\vb{x}}_i},
    \end{equation}
    so the rotational invariance of \(L\) gives the total angular momentum \(\vb{L} = \sum_{i = 1}^{n} m_i \vb{x}_i \cross \dot{\vb{x}}_i\) is conserved.
\end{example}

\begin{figure}[ht!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{fig/rotate-coordinates.png}
    \caption{Rotating the Coordinate System}
    \label{fig:rotate-coordinates}%
\end{figure}

In general, if the Lagrangian is independent for some (generalised) coordinates \(q^b\) with \(\pdv*{L}{q^b} = 0\), this coordinate is called \term{ignorable}, or \term{cyclic}, and the corresponding generalised momentum \(\pdv*{L}{\dot{q}^b}\) is conserved.

\begin{example}[Conservation of Energy]
    Suppose our Lagrangian has no explicit time dependence, i.e., \(\pdv*{L}{t} = 0\). Then we can translate \(t \mapsto t + \epsilon\), and
    \begin{equation}
        L\pqty{q^a \pqty{t + \epsilon}, \dot{q}^a \pqty{t + \epsilon}} = L\pqty{q^a\pqty{t} + \epsilon \dot{q}^a \pqty{t}, \dot{q}^a\pqty{t} + \epsilon \ddot{a}^a \pqty{t}} + \bigO\pqty{\epsilon^2},
    \end{equation}
    and hence
    \begin{equation}
        \rightbar{\pdv{L}{\epsilon}}_{\epsilon = 0} = \pdv{L}{q^a} \dot{q}^a + \pdv{L}{\dot{q}^a} \ddot{q}^a = \dv{L}{t}.
    \end{equation}

    This is a example of a symmetry, where \(\eta^a \pqty{t} = \dot{q}^a \pqty{t}\) and \(f = L\). Then, by Noether's Theorem, we have a conserved quantity
    \begin{equation}
        E = \dot{q}^a \pdv{L}{\dot{q}^a} - L
    \end{equation}
    is conserved, whenever the equations of motions hold. In the case where \(L = T - V\), then  \(E = T + V\) is the usual total energy.
\end{example}

The relation between symmetries of Lagrangian and conserved quantities was one of the most powerful ideas in the 20th century physics, but usually what happens is that experimentalists conducted particle experiments and observed that decay products have certain rules, and assigned new properties (e.g. strangeness/lepton number) to particles, and claim that they are conserved, which would explain that certain decay products are forbidden. Then, the underlying Lagrangian that describes the physics laws must have a symmetry that gives rise to this conserved quantity (we have not proved this, but this is indeed a one-to-one correspondence). The form of the Lagrangian are therefore restricted by such symmetries, which, generalised in many ways, is most of what 20th century physics is about.

\subsection{Constrained Motions and Conservation Laws}

\begin{figure}[ht!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{fig/pendulum-on-moving-support.png}
    \caption{Pendulum on a Moving Support}
    \label{fig:pendulum-on-moving-support}%
\end{figure}

\begin{example}[Pendulum on a Moving Support]
    Refer to \autoref{fig:pendulum-on-moving-support}. Suppose a mass \(M\) is attached to a support, free to move along a horizontal line, and a pendulum of length \(l\) is attached between it and a second mass \(m\), with coordinates \(\pqty{x, y}\) and angle \(\theta\) from vertical. Suppose \(M\) is \(X\) from some horizontal origin. Then the physical constraints give
    \begin{subequations}
        \begin{align}
            x &= X + l \sin \theta \\
            y &= - l \cos \theta
        \end{align}
    \end{subequations}

    The total kinetic energy of the system is therefore given by the sum of the two kinetic energies,
    \begin{equation}
        T = \frac{1}{2} M \dot{X}^2 + \frac{1}{2} m \pqty{\dot{x}^2 + \dot{y}^2}
    \end{equation}
    while the potential energy is \(V = mgy\). Similarly, it is easy to solve the constraint (\(\theta\)), and implicitly we have solved that \(Y\) is constant for \(M\). So the Lagrangian for the constrained motion is given by
    \begin{subequations}
        \begin{align}
            L &= \rightbar{T - V}_{\text{constraints}} \\
            &= \frac{1}{2} M \dot{X}^2 + \frac{1}{2} m \pqty{\pqty{\dot{X} + l \dot{\theta} \cos \theta}^2 + l^2 \dot{\theta}^2 \sin^2 \theta} + m g l \cos \theta \\
            &= \frac{1}{2} \pqty{M + m} \dot{X}^2 + m l \cos \theta \dot{X} \dot{\theta} + \frac{1}{2} m l^2 \dot{\theta}^2 + m g l \cos \theta.
        \end{align}
    \end{subequations}
    

    Clearly, \(L\) does not have explicit time dependence, so energy is conserved. In addition, \(L\) does not have explicit \(X\) dependence, so the momentum
    \begin{equation}
        p_X = \pdv{L}{\dot{X}} = \pqty{M + m} \dot{X} + m l \cos \theta \dot{\theta}
    \end{equation}
    is also conserved.

    For the \(\theta\) coordinate, by the Euler--Lagrange equations,
    \begin{subequations}
        \begin{align}
            0 &= \dv{t} \pdv{L}{\dot{\theta}} - \pdv{L}{\theta} \\
            &= \dv{t} \pqty{m l \cos \theta \dot{X} + m l^2 \dot{\theta}} + m l \sin \theta \dot{X} \dot{\theta} + m g l \sin \theta \\
            &= m l \cos \theta \ddot{X} + m l^2 \ddot{\theta} + m g l \sin \theta,
        \end{align}
    \end{subequations}
    and hence
    \begin{equation}
        0 = m l \cos \theta \ddot{x} + m l \dot{\theta}^2 + m g l \sin \theta.
    \end{equation}

    Since \(p_X\) is constant, by differentiating with respect to time, we have
    \begin{equation}
        \ddot{X} = \frac{ml}{M + m} \pqty{\sin \theta \dot{\theta}^2 - \cos \theta \ddot{\theta}},
    \end{equation}
    so the equation for \(\theta\pqty{t}\) reduces to
    \begin{equation}
        \label{eq:pendulum-on-moving-support-theta-eom}%
        \pqty{M + m \sin^2 \theta} \ddot{\theta} + m \sin \theta \cos \theta \dot{\theta}^2 + \frac{g}{l} \pqty{M + m} \sin \theta = 0.
    \end{equation}

    This looks slightly unpleasant, but we have the conservation of energy, so
    \begin{equation}
        E = \frac{1}{2} \pqty{M + m} \dot{X}^2 + ml \cos \theta \dot{\theta} \dot{X} + \frac{1}{2} m l^2 \dot{\theta}^2 - mgl \cos \theta
    \end{equation}
    is constant, and using the conserved momentum \(p_X\), this becomes
    \begin{equation}
        E = \frac{p_M^2}{2 \pqty{M + m}} + \frac{m l^2}{2 \pqty{M + m}} \pqty{M + m \sin^2 \theta} \dot{\theta}^2 - m g l \cos \theta
    \end{equation}
    which is the first integral of the \(\theta\) equation. (Differentiating this with respect to time recovers the \(\theta\) equation, in \autoref{eq:pendulum-on-moving-support-theta-eom}.) If we write this in the form \(\dv*{\theta}{t} = g\pqty{\theta}\) for some function \(g\pqty{\theta}\), then
    \begin{equation}
        t - t_0 = \int_{\theta\pqty{t_0}}^{\theta\pqty{t}} \frac{\dd{\theta}}{g\pqty{\theta}}
    \end{equation}
    is the (rather implicit) solution to the equations of motion. We say the solution is \term{reduced to quadrature}.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}
    It is important to first obtain \emph{all} equations of motions, and then simplify using conserved quantities, e.g. it would \emph{not} be correct to first find \(\dot{X} = \dot{X}\pqty{p_X, \theta, \dot{\theta}}\) and substitute this back into \(L\) before finding \(\theta\) equation of motion. This is \emph{incorrect}, since the conservation laws are only valid along the true trajectory, and not for all paths. Then if we substitute this back into \(L\), we are effectively restricting the set of paths to only those that satisfy the conservation law, which is not the set of all paths, and hence the Euler--Lagrange equations will not be valid.
\end{remark}

\begin{example}[Spherical Pendulum]
    We setup a spherical pendulum as illustrated in \autoref{fig:spherical-pendulum}. The location of the mass (with respect to the centre of sphere) is given by
    \begin{subequations}
        \begin{align}
            x &= l \sin \theta \cos \phi \\
            y &= l \sin \theta \sin \phi \\
            z &= - l \cos \theta
        \end{align}
    \end{subequations}
    so the constraint Lagrangian will be given by
    \begin{equation}
        L = \frac{1}{2} m l^2 \pqty{\dot{\theta}^2 + \sin^2 \theta \dot{\phi}^2} + m g l \cos \theta.
    \end{equation}

    Here, \(\phi\) is ignorable with \(\pdv{L}{\phi} = 0\), so the generalised momentum
    \begin{equation}
        p_\phi = \pdv{L}{\dot{\phi}} = m l^2 \sin^2 \theta \dot{\phi}
    \end{equation}
    is conserved. This is the angular momentum in the \(\phi\) direction (about the vertical axis). Energy is conserved since there is no explicit time dependence:
    \begin{equation}
        E = \frac{1}{2} m l^2 \pqty{\dot{\theta}^2 + \sin^2 \theta \dot{\phi}^2} - m g l \cos \theta.
    \end{equation}

    Putting this together, we have
    \begin{equation}
        E = \frac{1}{2} m l^2 \dot{\theta}^2 + \frac{p_\phi^2}{2 m l^2 \sin^2\theta} - m g l \cos \theta,
    \end{equation}
    which is the first integral of the \(\theta\) equation of motion.

    We may write this in terms of an effective potential \(V_{\text{eff}}\pqty{\theta}\) as
    \begin{equation}
        V_{\text{eff}} \pqty{\theta} = \frac{p_\phi^2}{2 m l^2 \sin^2 \theta} - m g l \cos \theta
    \end{equation}
    with \(E = \frac{1}{2} m l^2 \dot{\theta}^2 + V_{\text{eff}}\pqty{\theta}\). Hence, we have
    \begin{equation}
        \dv{\theta}{t} = \sqrt{\frac{2 \pqty{E - V_{\text{eff}}\pqty{\theta}}}{m l^2}}
    \end{equation}
    which reduces to a quadrature.

    Although this is very tough to solve, we can try to understand the motion by plotting \(V_{\text{eff}} \pqty{\theta}\), assuming \(p_\phi \neq 0\). For a fixed \(p_{\phi}\), \(V_{\text{eff}}\) looks quadratic, and the motion will be confined within some range of \(\oointv{\theta_0}{\theta_1}\) where \(V_{\text{eff}}\pqty{\theta_0} = V_{\text{eff}}\pqty{\theta_1} = E\), as demonstrated in \autoref{fig:spherical-pendulum-effective-potential} -- there is a ``centrifugal barrier'' term here, just like planetary circular motion. The ball will therefore ``bob up and down'' between the two angles, as illustrated in \autoref{fig:spherical-pendulum-motion}.
\end{example}

\subsection{Charged Particle in an Electromagnetic Field}

Consider a particle of mass \(m\) and charge \(e\) moving in an electromagnetic field
\begin{equation}
    \vb{E} = - \grad{\phi} - \pdv{\vb{A}}{t} \qand \vb{B} = \curl{\vb{A}}
\end{equation}
where \(\phi = \phi\pqty{\vb{x}, t}\) is the scalar potential and \(\vb{A} = \vb{A} \pqty{\vb{x}, t}\) is the vector potential. If our particle is moving with velocity \(\vb{v}\) much less than the speed of light with \(\abs{\vb{v}} \ll c\), then the Lagrangian for the particle is given by
\begin{equation}
    L = \frac{1}{2} m \dot{\vb{x}}^2 - e \phi + e \dot{\vb{x}} \vdot \vb{A}.
\end{equation}
and the Euler--Lagrange equations give
\begin{equation}
    0 = \dv{t} \pqty{\pdv{L}{\dot{\vb{x}}}} - \pdv{L}{\vb{x}} = \dv{t} \pqty{m \dot{\vb{x}} + e \vb{A}} + e \pqty{\grad \phi - \grad \pqty{\dot{\vb{x}} \vdot \vb{A}}}.
\end{equation}

Hence,
\begin{equation}
    m \ddot{x}^a = -e \pqty{\pdv{\phi}{x^a} + \pdv{A_a}{t}} + e \pqty{\pdv{A_b}{x^a} - \pdv{A_a}{x^b}} \dot{x}^b
\end{equation}
which indeed yields
\begin{equation}
    m \ddot{\vb{x}} = e \pqty{\vb{E} + \dot{\vb{x}} \cross \vb{B}}
\end{equation}
which is the \term{Lorentz force law}.

\begin{remarks}
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item Note the generalised momentum is different from the classical momentum, with
        \begin{equation}
            \vb{p} = \pdv{L}{\dot{\vb{x}}} = m \dot{\vb{x}} + e \vb{A}
        \end{equation}
        also having a contribution from the vector potential, and in particular is not just \(m \vb{v}\).
        \item The Lagrangian depends on the potentials \(\pqty{\phi, \vb{A}}\) rather than the fields \(\vb{E}, \vb{B}\) themselves. These potentials have \term{gauge redundancy}, in that the Lagrangian is invariant under the \term{gauge transformation}
        \begin{subequations}
            \begin{align}
                \vb{A} &\mapsto \vb{A} + \grad f \\
                \phi &\mapsto \phi - \pdv{f}{t}
            \end{align}
        \end{subequations}
        for some \(f = \pqty{\vb{x}, t}\), which leaves the fields \(\vb{E}, \vb{B}\) invariant. Under this gauge transformation, we have
        \begin{equation}
            L \mapsto L + e \pqty{\pdv{f}{t} + \dot{\vb{x}} \vdot \grad f} = L + e \dv{f}{t},
        \end{equation}
        so the variational problem with fixed endpoints is unchanged.
        \item In special relativity, with the Minkowski metric taking convention
        \begin{equation}
            \tensor{\eta}{_{\mu\nu}} = \text{diag}\pqty{-1, 1, 1, 1},
        \end{equation}
        we have
        \begin{equation}
            L = m \sqrt{- \tensor{\eta}{_{\mu \nu}} \tensor{\dot{x}}{^\mu} \tensor{\dot{x}}{^\nu}} - e \tensor{A}{_\mu} \tensor{\dot{x}}{^\mu}
        \end{equation}
        where we have the 4-vector 
        \begin{equation}
            \tensor{x}{^\mu} = \pqty{t\pqty{\tau}, \vb{x}\pqty{\tau}}
        \end{equation}
        is parametrised by proper time \(\tau\), and the 4-vector potential
        \begin{equation}
            \tensor{A}{_\mu} = \pqty{\phi, -\vb{A}}.
        \end{equation}
    \end{enumerate}
\end{remarks}

\subsection{Small Oscillations}

Having set up the Lagrangian \(L\) for some physical motion, we often want to identify the \term{points of equilibrium}, then identify whether or not such points of equilibrium is \term{stable}.

Consider a general Lagrangian in the form
\begin{equation}
    L = \frac{1}{2} T_{ab} \dot{q}^a \dot{q}^b - V\pqty{q}
\end{equation}
that is quadratic in velocities, where \(T\) is real, symmetric and positive-definite. Then the Euler--Lagrange equations read
\begin{equation}
    \dv{t} \pqty{T_{ab} \dot{q}^b} = \frac{1}{2} \pdv{T_{bc}}{q^a} \dot{q}^b \dot{q}^c - \pdv{V}{q^a}.
\end{equation}

A \term{point of equilibrium} is a solution of the Euler--Lagrange equations of the form \(\dot{q}^a \pqty{t} = 0\) for all \(a\) and \(t\). This can only occur at the critical points of the potential, where \(\pdv*{V}{q^a} = 0\) for all \(a\).

Without loss of generality, choose the generalised coordinates such that \(q^a\pqty{t} = 0\) is a point of equilibrium. To determine stability, we expand about the point of equilibrium, getting
\begin{subequations}
    \begin{align}
        T &= \frac{1}{2} T_{ab} \pqty{0} \dot{q}^a \dot{q}^b + \bigO\pqty{q^3, \dot{q}^3} \\
        V &= V\pqty{0} + \frac{1}{2} \pdv{V}{q^a}{q^b} \pqty{0} q^a q^b + \bigO\pqty{q^3}
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}
where we treat both the velocities \(\dot{q}^a\) and departures from equilibrium as small. We can drop \(V\pqty{0}\) since it is a constant, so the dynamics near an equilibrium point are determined by quadratic Lagrangian
\begin{equation}
    L^{\pqty{2}} = \frac{1}{2} \mathcal{T}_{ab} \dot{q}^a \dot{q}^b - \frac{1}{2} \mathcal{V}_{ab} q^a q^b
\end{equation}
where we denote \(\mathcal{T}_{ab} = T_{ab}\pqty{0}\) and \(\mathcal{V}_{ab} = \pdv*{V}{q^a}{q^b}\pqty{0}\). The Euler--Lagrange equations for this quadratic Lagrangian then reads
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{T} \ddot{\vb{q}} + \mathcal{V} \vb{q} = \vb{0}
\end{equation}
for real. symmetric matrices \(T\) and \(V\). In physical situations, \(\mathcal{T}\) is positive-definite and hence invertible, and the equations of motion are therefore
\begin{equation}
    \ddot{\vb{q}} = - \mathcal{T}\inverse \mathcal{V} \vb{q}.
\end{equation}

We seek solutions of the form \(\vb{q}\pqty{t} = f\pqty{t} \vb*{\alpha}\) for some constant vector \(\vb*{\alpha}\). With this ansatz, we have
\begin{equation}
    \ddot{f}\pqty{t} \mathcal{T} \vb*{\alpha} = - f\pqty{t} \mathcal{V} \vb*{\alpha}.
\end{equation}

Since \(\mathcal{T}\) is invertible, for \(\vb*{\alpha}\) non-zero, we always have \(\mathcal{T} \vb*{\alpha} \neq \vb{0}\), and hence
\begin{equation}
    \ddot{f} = - \lambda f \qwhere \pqty{\lambda \mathcal{T} - \mathcal{V}} \vb*{\alpha} = \vb{0}
\end{equation}
for some constant \(\lambda\). Hence, \(\det \pqty{\lambda \mathcal{T} - \mathcal{V}} = 0\).

If this characteristic polynomial has degree \(n\), there will be \(n\) roots \(\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_n\) and the solution will be \(\vb{q}\pqty{t} = f_r\pqty{t} \vb*{\alpha}_r\), where \(\vb*{\alpha}_r\) is the corresponding eigenvector satisfying \(\pqty{\lambda_r \mathcal{T} - \mathcal{V}} \vb*{\alpha}_r = \vb{0}\), and \(\ddot{f}_r = - \lambda_r f_r\).

In fact, all the roots \(\lambda_r\) are real in this case. This is because both \(\mathcal{T}\) and \(\mathcal{V}\) are real and symmetric. For each \(r\), we have
\begin{equation}
    \lambda_r \vb*{\alpha}_r \hermitian \mathcal{T} \vb*{\alpha}_r = \vb*{\alpha}_r \hermitian \mathcal{V} \vb*{\alpha}_r
\end{equation}
and subtracting its complex conjugate, we know
\begin{equation}
    \pqty{\lambda_r - \lambda_r\conjstar} \vb*{\alpha}_r \hermitian \mathcal{T} \vb*{\alpha}_r = 0,
\end{equation}
so \(\lambda_r\) is real since \(\vb*{\alpha}_r \hermitian \mathcal{T} \vb*{\alpha}_r > 0\).

If \(\lambda_r > 0\), then \(f_r\pqty{t} = A_R \cos\pqty{\omega_r t + \phi_r}\), where \(\omega_r = \sqrt{\lambda_r}\) is the \term{(angular) frequency} of the oscillation, and \(A_r\) is the \term{amplitude}.

If however, some \(\lambda_r < 0\) is negative, then the corresponding \(f_r\pqty{t}\) grows exponentially, and our quadratic approximation for \(L^{\pqty{2}}\) is no longer valid, so the equilibrium point is \term{unstable}.

If some \(\lambda_r = 0\), then the corresponding \(f_r\pqty{t} = a_r + b_r t\), and we need to examine higher order terms to determine the stability.

If \(\lambda_r > 0\) for \emph{every} \(r\), then the equilibrium point is \term{stable}, and the collection of associated eigenvectors \(\Bqty{\vb*{\alpha}_r}\) are called the \term{normal modes} of the system. A general solution near this equilibrium point \(q^a = 0\) will be a superposition of normal modes.

\begin{example}[Double Pendulum]
    Refer to \autoref{fig:double-pendulum}. In general, the (constrained) Lagrangian of the system is given by
    \begin{equation}
        L = m l^2 \dot{\theta}_1^2 + \frac{1}{2} m l^2 \dot{\theta}_2^2 + m l^2 \cos\pqty{\theta_2 - \theta_1} \dot{\theta}_1 \dot{\theta}_2 + m g l \pqty{2 \cos \theta_1 + \cos \theta_2}.
    \end{equation}

    Clearly, there is an equilibrium when \(\theta_1 = \theta_2 = 0\), and we can expand about this point to get the quadratic Lagrangian
    \begin{equation}
        L^{\pqty{2}} = m l^2 \pqty{\dot{\theta}_1^2 + \frac{1}{2} \dot{\theta}_2^2 + \dot{\theta}_1 \dot{\theta}_2} - m g l \pqty{\theta_1^2 + \frac{1}{2} \theta_2^2},
    \end{equation}
    and the matrices are given by
    \begin{equation}
        \mathcal{T} = m l^2 \pmqty{2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1} \qand \mathcal{V} = m g l \pmqty{2 & 0 \\ 0 & 1}.
    \end{equation}

    Then the characteristic polynomial is given by
    \begin{subequations}
        \begin{align}
            \det\pqty{\lambda \mathcal{T} - \mathcal{V}} &= \vmqty{2 ml^2 \lambda - 2 m g l & ml^2 \lambda \\ ml^2 \lambda & ml^2 \lambda - m g l} \\
            &= m^2 l^4 \bqty{2 \pqty{\lambda - \frac{g}{l}}^2 - \lambda^2},
        \end{align}
    \end{subequations}
    so the eigenvalues are
    \begin{equation}
        \lambda_1 = \pqty{2 - \sqrt{2}} \frac{g}{l} \qand \lambda_2 = \pqty{2 + \sqrt{2}} \frac{g}{l}
    \end{equation}
    with corresponding eigenvectors
    \begin{equation}
        \vb*{\alpha}_1 = \pmqty{1 \\ \sqrt{2}} \qand \vb*{\alpha}_2 = \pmqty{1 \\ -\sqrt{2}}.
    \end{equation}

    Notice that both eigenvalues are positive, so the equilibrium is stable. The normal modes are illustrated as below.

    Note that the normal mode with the lower frequency (\(\lambda_1 < \lambda_2\)) has oscillations in phase, as in \autoref{fig:double-pendulum-normal-mode-in-phase}, whereas the normal mode with the higher frequency (\(\lambda_2 > \lambda_1\)) has oscillations out of phase, as in \autoref{fig:double-pendulum-normal-mode-out-of-phase}.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Linear Triatomic Molecule]
    For a molecule such as carbon dioxide, we may model it as three collinear masses joined by identical springs, as in \autoref{fig:linear-triatomic-molecule}.

    Then the Lagrangian is already quadratic, with
    \begin{equation}
        L = L^{\pqty{2}} = \frac{1}{2} m \pqty{\dot{x}_1^2 + \dot{x}_3^2} + \frac{1}{2} M \dot{x}_2^2 - \frac{1}{2} k \pqty{\pqty{x_1 - x_2}^2 + \pqty{x_2 - x_3}^2}.
    \end{equation}

    So the matrices are given by
    \begin{equation}
        \mathcal{T} = \pmqty{m & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & M & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & m} \qand \mathcal{V} = k \pmqty{1 & -1 & 0 \\ -1 & 2 & -1 \\ 0 & -1 & 1}.
    \end{equation}

    Computing the determinant, we get
    \begin{subequations}
        \begin{align}
            \det\pqty{\lambda \mathcal{T} - \mathcal{V}} &= \vmqty{\lambda m - k & 0 & k \\ 0 & \lambda M - 2 k & k \\ k & k & \lambda m - k} \\
            &= \lambda \pqty{\lambda m - k} \pqty{\lambda m M - k \pqty{2m + M}}.
        \end{align}
    \end{subequations}

    The three eigenvalues and eigenvectors are as follows.

    \begin{itemize}
        \item \(\lambda_1 = 0\) with \(\vb*{\alpha}_1 = \pmqty{1 \\ 1 \\ 1}\), which represents uniform transformation, and there is no relative motion between the masses, as in \autoref{fig:linear-triatomic-molecule-normal-mode-translational}.
        \item \(\lambda_2 = \flatfrac{k}{m}\) with \(\vb*{\alpha}_2 = \pmqty{1 \\ 0 \\ -1}\), which represents the two outer masses oscillating in opposite directions (out of phase), while the middle mass is stationary, as in \autoref{fig:linear-triatomic-molecule-normal-mode-stationary-middle}.
        \item \(\lambda_3 = \flatfrac{k}{m} \pqty{1 + \pqty{\flatfrac{2 m}{M}}}\) with \(\vb*{\alpha}_3 = \pmqty{1 \\ -\frac{2 m}{M} \\ 1}\), which represents the two outer masses oscillating in phase, while the middle mass oscillates in opposite direction, as in \autoref{fig:linear-triatomic-molecule-normal-mode-oscillating-middle}.
    \end{itemize}
\end{example}
